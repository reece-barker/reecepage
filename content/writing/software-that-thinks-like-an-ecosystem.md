My slogan is "Software that thinks like an ecosystem". It replaced an older one, and it is worth saying where it comes
from, because it is not just a nice sounding line. It is built on one sentence from a book published in 1962, and on a
handful of other books that say the same thing.

**The short version:** you cannot change one part of a living system and expect the rest to stand still, and software
is part of a living system whether we design it that way or not.

## One sentence from Silent Spring

Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) is about pesticides on the surface. Underneath, it is about following one thing
through everything it touches: the chemical in the soil, the water, the insects, the birds and in the end the people.
The line my slogan comes from is a short one, at the end of her chapter on water:

> Here again we are reminded that in nature nothing exists alone.[1]

She has just shown how arsenic from poisoned soil is carried by rain into streams, reservoirs and groundwater. The
sentence is her summing up. It is usually quoted without its first half, as "In nature nothing exists alone", and that
is the form most people will know.

Where to find it: Chapter 4, "Surface Waters and Underground Seas", near the end, around page 51 depending on the edition.[1]

## Muir said it fifty years earlier

John Muir came at the same idea from the other side, from walking in the Sierra Nevada. In *My First Summer in the
Sierra* (1911) he wrote:

> When we try to pick out anything by itself, we find it hitched to everything else in the Universe.[2]

It is chapter 6 in the Sierra Club's edition, on page 110.[2] The wording has its own story. Muir first wrote the idea
in his journal in 1869, in a longer and stranger form, that things are "bound fast by a thousand invisible cords that
cannot be broken". He tightened it into the "hitched" sentence when he turned the journal into the book, more than forty
years later.[2]

Carson came at it with data from a laboratory, Muir with his boots on a mountain. They end up in the same place.

## The same idea in three more books

Once I started looking, it was in most of the serious writing on sustainability, each time in different words.

- **Aldo Leopold, *A Sand County Almanac* (1949).** The last essay, "The Land Ethic", asks us to widen the idea of a
  community to include soil, water, plants and animals, and to treat them as members of it and not as property.[3]
- **William McDonough and Michael Braungart, *Cradle to Cradle* (2002).** Design things so that nothing is waste:
  what is left over becomes food for something else, the way it does in a forest.[4]
- **Donella Meadows, *Thinking in Systems* (2008) and her essay "Leverage Points" (1999).** A system is more than its
  parts, and the place where you push is often not the place where it changes. Her essay ranks twelve places to
  intervene in a system, from the least effective, like tweaking numbers, to the most, like changing the goal the whole
  system is working towards.[5]

Different decades, different jobs, one habit of mind: look at the whole thing, not just the part in front of you.

## Why this is about software

Software is not weightless. It runs on hardware that was mined and built, it uses electricity every second it is on,
and it ends up as waste when the hardware is thrown away. A feature changes the people who use it, the people who look
after it and the machines underneath it. That is an ecosystem, whether we design it like one or not.

So when I say software should think like an ecosystem, this is what I mean by it in practice:

- Every part has a job and the parts depend on each other in the open, not by accident.
- Nothing runs that does not need to run.
- When one part fails, the rest keep working.
- Waste is a design problem, not something to deal with later.

That is the standard I want to build to, and I would rather put it on my business cards and hold myself to it.

## Read more

If you want to follow the idea yourself, these are free or easy to find:

- Carson's own website has a page on the book and her work: [rachelcarson.org, Silent Spring](https://rachelcarson.org/silent-spring).
- *My First Summer in the Sierra* is in the public domain, so the whole book is free to read: [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/32540).
- The Sierra Club's page on how Muir's best known lines are often misquoted, including this one: [John Muir Misquoted](https://www.sierraclub.org/john-muir-exhibit/john-muir-misquoted).
- The Aldo Leopold Foundation explains the land ethic in plain words: [Understanding the land ethic](https://aldoleopold.org/blogs/understanding-the-land-ethic).
- Meadows' leverage points essay is short and free: [donellameadows.org](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/).
- William McDonough's own writing on the cradle to cradle idea: [The Cradle-to-Cradle Alternative](https://mcdonough.com/writings/cradle-cradle-alternative/).
