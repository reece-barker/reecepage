My slogan is "Software that thinks like an ecosystem". I swapped it in for an older one, and it is worth saying
where it comes from, because it is not just a nice sounding line.

## One sentence from Silent Spring

Rachel Carson published *Silent Spring* in 1962. On the surface it is a book about pesticides. Underneath it is a book
about following one thing through everything else it touches: the chemical in the soil, the water, the insects, the
birds and in the end the people. Somewhere in it is the line I keep coming back to:

> In nature nothing exists alone.

You cannot change one part of a living system and expect the rest to stand still. That sounds obvious until you
count how many things get built exactly as if it were not true.

## Muir said it fifty years earlier

John Muir wrote the same idea from the other side, from walking in the Sierra Nevada. In *My First Summer in the
Sierra* (1911) he put it like this:

> When we try to pick out anything by itself, we find it hitched to everything else in the Universe.

Carson came at it with data from a laboratory, Muir with his boots on a mountain. They end up in the same place.

## The same idea in other books

Once I started looking, it was everywhere in the writing on sustainability, each time in different words.

- **Aldo Leopold, *A Sand County Almanac* (1949).** His "land ethic" asks us to widen the idea of a community to include
  soil, water, plants and animals, and to treat them as members and not as property.
- **Donella Meadows, *Thinking in Systems* (2008).** A system is more than its parts, and the place where you push
  is often not the place where it changes. Her best known essay on this is about leverage points.
- **William McDonough and Michael Braungart, *Cradle to Cradle* (2002).** Design things so that nothing is waste:
  what is left over becomes food for something else, the way it does in a forest.

Different decades, different jobs, one habit of mind: look at the whole thing, not just the part in front of you.

## Why this is about software

Software is not weightless. It runs on hardware that was mined and built, it uses electricity every second it is on,
and it ends up as waste when the hardware is thrown away. A feature changes the people who use it, the people who
look after it and the machines underneath it. That is an ecosystem, whether we design it like one or not.

So when I say software should think like an ecosystem, this is what I mean by it in practice:

- Every part has a job and the parts depend on each other in the open, not by accident.
- Nothing runs that does not need to run.
- When one part fails, the rest keep working.
- Waste is a design problem, not something to deal with later.

That is the standard I want to build to, and I would rather put it on my business cards and hold myself to it.
